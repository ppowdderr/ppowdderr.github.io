There is a quiet vanity in the reading of many books. The count is visible, the covers accumulate, and the mind is flattered by motion. Rereading offers none of this. It looks, from the outside, like standing still. You return to a book you have already finished, and there is nothing to report at the end of it except that you have been there before and understood less than you thought.

That, of course, is the whole point. A first reading is mostly reconnaissance: you are learning the shape of the thing, following the plot or the argument, and you are far too busy staying oriented to notice how anything is made. The sentences pass under you like ground under a train. The second reading is the first honest one. Knowing where the road goes, you can finally look out of the window.

I find, too, that a book reread is a measuring instrument pointed at me rather than at itself. The text has not changed a word. Whatever is different — the passages that now seem obvious, the ones that suddenly cut, the joke I missed entirely — is a record of what has happened to me in the interval. Few things show you your own drift as precisely as a paragraph you once underlined for reasons you can no longer reconstruct.

This is not an argument against new books; a mind fed only on its own favourites goes stale in a particular and recognisable way. It is an argument against treating reading as intake. Three books held closely will do more to you than thirty passed through. The library that matters is not the one on the shelf but the small handful you can still hear when you are not reading at all.
