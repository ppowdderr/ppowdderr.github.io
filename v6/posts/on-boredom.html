Boredom has a bad name, and it earned some of it honestly. It is unpleasant, it makes the hours thick, and it arrives most reliably at the moments we have least patience for it. But I have started to suspect that boredom is not a defect in the day. It is a signal, and like most signals we dislike, we have learned to silence it faster than we can read it. The phone is not an escape from boredom so much as a way of never receiving its message.

What is the message? Usually something unflattering: that the thing in front of me is not worth my attention, or worse, that I am not currently capable of paying attention to something that is. Both readings are useful and both are avoided. A bored mind is an idle instrument asking to be pointed somewhere. If it is never allowed to remain idle, it is never allowed to ask.

I have noticed that my better ideas do not arrive during work. They arrive in the twenty unstructured minutes afterwards, when nothing is demanded and the mind, having nowhere to go, begins to wander through its own rooms. This is not mysticism; it is what happens when the foreground finally empties enough for the background to be heard. The cost of filling every gap is not that we lose the gaps. It is that we lose what would have grown in them.

So I have been practising something small and slightly ridiculous: sitting with the dull minute instead of ending it. Waiting for a kettle without reaching for anything. Walking without a voice in my ear. The first week it feels like a punishment. By the second it feels like getting a room back that someone else had been renting.

None of this is an argument for a duller life. It is an argument that the boredom we keep fleeing is the price of admission for the only kind of thinking that has ever been worth anything to me — slow, unprompted, and mine.
