A ship at open sea needs very little from anyone. Given a compass, a chart and the stars, a captain can cross an ocean alone, and for most of history many did. The open sea is, perhaps surprisingly, the safer part of the voyage; there is nothing out there to hit. Ships are rarely lost in the middle of the ocean. They are lost near the coast, in the last few miles, where the water is shallow, the rocks are hidden, and the captain, having been right for a thousand miles, is at his most confident. It's in these miles that ships are not trusted to find their own way. They are shown it. I believe the same holds for people, and I will now explain why being shown the way is not a weakness of the voyage, but the way voyages are meant to end.

Consider first the compass. A ship's compass does not point to true north. It points to magnetic north, which is off by an amount that changes with place and year, and it is pulled further off by the ship's own iron: its hull, its engines, its cargo. Sailors call this deviation, and it cannot be found by staring harder at the compass. It is found from outside, by turning the ship against marks on land whose bearings are known, and writing the error down. One's judgement works the same way. It points roughly in the right direction, and it is bent by what one carries (fears, ambitions, what one would like to be true). The error is real, and it can only be measured against something fixed that isn't oneself.

Then there is the lighthouse. It is easy to think a lighthouse marks home. It doesn't; it marks danger, a reef or a headland, and tells a ship where it is relative to it. Every light flashes in its own pattern so that one light can be told from another, but only by a sailor who already has the pattern in his chart. A light one cannot read is just a light. So it is with the people and principles that guide us: they are of no use to someone who never learned to read them, and the learning has to happen before the night it is needed. Note also that the lighthouse never moves towards the ship. It stays on its rock through every storm, for ships it will never meet, and asks nothing of them but to look.

Last is the pilot. At the mouth of many harbours, a local pilot climbs a rope ladder onto the ship and takes her in, because he knows the sandbanks, the currents and the shifting channels better than any visiting captain could. Many ports require it by law, and no good captain takes it as an insult. He remains the captain; he simply admits that knowing the ocean is not the same as knowing this harbour. Most of us cross our open seas well enough on our own. It's the arrivals, into a new place, a new craft, a new family, where we run aground, and where the pride of a long voyage tempts us to refuse the pilot.

To be shown one's way home, then, is to correct one's compass against what is fixed, to learn the lights before the dark, and to let the pilot aboard. None of this makes the voyage any less one's own. And the analogy completes itself in a way I find comforting: most pilots were once captains themselves. Having been shown the way in, they stay ashore and show others. A ship that is guided home becomes, in time, part of the harbour.
